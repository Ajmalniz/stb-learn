Section A — Multiple Choice Questions

Tick mark (✓) the correct answer.

1. In an atom the number of protons and neutrons are added to obtain:
A. Number of electrons
B. Number of nucleons
C. Atomic number of element
D. Number of isotopes
Protons and neutrons are together called nucleons because they live in the nucleus. So (protons + neutrons) = nucleon number (also called the mass number, A).
2. If the proton number is 19, the electronic configuration will be:
A. 2, 8, 9
B. 2, 8, 8, 1
C. 2, 8, 1
D. 2, 8, 3
Proton number 19 = potassium (K), which has 19 electrons. Filling shells (2, 8, 8, then 1): 2, 8, 8, 1. A shell cannot hold 9 electrons before the next one starts, so (a) is wrong.
3. If the nucleon number of potassium is 39 and its atomic number is 19, then the number of neutrons will be:
A. 39
B. 19
C. 20
D. 29
Neutrons = Nucleon number − Atomic number = 39 − 19 = 20.
4. The isotope C-12 is present in an abundance of:
A. 96.9%
B. 97.6%
C. 98.89%
D. 99.7%
Natural carbon is about 98.89% C-12 and 1.11% C-13 (with a trace of radioactive C-14). This is why the average atomic mass of carbon is close to 12.
5. Electronic configuration is the distribution of:
A. Protons
B. Neutrons
C. Electrons
D. Positrons
Electronic configuration describes how the electrons of an atom are arranged in shells and subshells around the nucleus.
6. Which one of the following is the most penetrating?
A. Electron
B. Proton
C. Alpha particle
D. Neutron
A neutron has no electric charge, so it is not slowed or deflected by the electric fields of atoms. It passes deep into matter, making it the most penetrating of these particles (a heavy, charged alpha particle is the least penetrating).
7. How many subshells are in an L shell?
A. One
B. Two
C. Three
D. Four
The L shell is the 2nd shell (n = 2). The number of subshells equals n, so it has two subshells: 2s and 2p.
8. De Broglie extended the wave–particle duality to the electron in:
A. 1920
B. 1922
C. 1923
D. 1925
Louis de Broglie proposed that matter (like the electron) has wave properties in 1923 (formally published in his 1924 thesis).
9. Name the material of the screen which is used in Rutherford's atomic model experiment:
A. Aluminium foil
B. Zinc sulphide
C. Sodium sulphide
D. Aluminium sulphide
Rutherford used a fluorescent zinc sulphide (ZnS) screen. It produces a tiny flash of light (scintillation) wherever an alpha particle strikes it, letting the scientists count the deflected particles.
10. Which rays are used for the sterilization of medical instruments?
A. α-rays
B. β-rays
C. γ-rays
D. X-rays
Gamma (γ) rays, usually from cobalt-60, are highly penetrating and kill bacteria and germs, so they are used to sterilise surgical instruments, syringes, and dressings.

Section B — Short Questions

Q1. Draw the structure of the isotopes of chlorine to justify the definition of isotopes.

Definition: Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same atomic number (protons) but a different nucleon number (mass number) because they have different numbers of neutrons.

Chlorine has two natural isotopes: Cl-35 (₁₇Cl³⁵) and Cl-37 (₁₇Cl³⁷).

Isotope Protons (p⁺) Neutrons (n⁰) Electrons (e⁻) Configuration
Cl-35 (₁₇Cl³⁵) 17 35 − 17 = 18 17 2, 8, 7
Cl-37 (₁₇Cl³⁷) 17 37 − 17 = 20 17 2, 8, 7

Justification: Both atoms have the same 17 protons and 17 electrons (so both are chlorine with identical chemical behaviour), but they differ in neutrons (18 vs 20) and therefore in mass number (35 vs 37). Same protons + different neutrons = isotopes. ✔

Q2. An atom has 5 electrons in its M shell. Then: (a) find its atomic number, (b) write the electronic configuration, (c) name the element.

The M shell is the 3rd shell. Before electrons can enter the M shell, the K shell (2) and L shell (8) must be filled.

(a) Atomic number: Electrons = K + L + M = 2 + 8 + 5 = 15. Since a neutral atom has protons = electrons, atomic number Z = 15.

(b) Electronic configuration: 2, 8, 5  (or 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³).

(c) Name of element: Element with atomic number 15 is Phosphorus (P).

Q3. Justify that Rutherford's atomic model has defects.

Rutherford correctly proposed a small, dense, positive nucleus with electrons around it, but his model had two major defects:

  • Instability of the atom (contradicts classical physics): According to classical electromagnetic theory, an electron moving in a circular path is accelerating, so it must continuously radiate (lose) energy. It would spiral inward and crash into the nucleus in a fraction of a second (≈ 10⁻⁸ s). But real atoms are stable — so the model fails to explain this.
  • Cannot explain the line spectrum: If the electron lost energy continuously, it should emit a continuous spectrum. But atoms actually give a discontinuous line spectrum (definite, separate lines). Rutherford's model could not explain these fixed spectral lines.
  • It also gave no information about the arrangement of electrons (their energy levels or distribution) around the nucleus.

These defects were later removed by Bohr's atomic model.

Q4. Describe the wave–particle duality of the electron in De Broglie's hypothesis.

In 1923, Louis de Broglie suggested that just as light shows both wave and particle nature, matter (like the electron) also has a dual nature — it can behave both as a particle and as a wave. This is called wave–particle duality.

He proposed that a moving particle of mass m and velocity v has an associated wavelength:

λ = h / mv   (where h = Planck's constant)

  • Large, heavy objects (a ball, a car) have extremely small wavelengths, so their wave nature is not noticed.
  • Tiny particles like the electron (very small mass) have a measurable wavelength, so their wave nature is significant.

This idea was later confirmed experimentally (electron diffraction) and became the foundation of the modern (quantum-mechanical) model of the atom.

Q5. What are the limitations of Bohr's atomic model?
  • Works only for one-electron systems: Bohr's model successfully explains the spectrum of hydrogen (and ions like He⁺) but fails for atoms having more than one electron.
  • Fine spectrum (Zeeman & Stark effects): It could not explain the splitting of spectral lines when the atom is placed in a magnetic field (Zeeman effect) or an electric field (Stark effect).
  • Ignores wave nature & uncertainty: It treated the electron only as a particle moving in a fixed circular orbit. This contradicts de Broglie's dual nature and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (we cannot know an electron's exact position and momentum at the same time).
  • Cannot explain shapes of orbits / chemical bonding: It gave no explanation for the three-dimensional shapes of orbitals or how atoms form bonds to make molecules.
Q6. Differentiate between a shell and a subshell with examples.
Shell (Energy Level) Subshell (Sub-energy Level)
A main energy level in which electrons revolve around the nucleus. A division within a shell; a shell is made up of one or more subshells.
Denoted by K, L, M, N… or n = 1, 2, 3, 4… Denoted by s, p, d, f.
Maximum electrons = 2n². Max electrons: s = 2, p = 6, d = 10, f = 14.

Example: L shell (n = 2) holds up to 8 electrons. Example: L shell contains 2s and 2p subshells.
Q7. How are the atoms of ₈O¹⁶ and ₈O¹⁸ similar to or different from each other?

₈O¹⁶ and ₈O¹⁸ are two isotopes of oxygen.

Similarities:

  • Same atomic number (Z = 8): both have 8 protons and 8 electrons.
  • Same electronic configuration (2, 6), so they have identical chemical properties.
  • Both are the same element — oxygen.

Differences:

  • Different nucleon (mass) numbers: 16 and 18.
  • Different number of neutrons: O-16 has 16 − 8 = 8 neutrons; O-18 has 18 − 8 = 10 neutrons.
  • Therefore they differ slightly in mass and physical properties (e.g. density).

Section C — Detailed Questions

Q1. Discuss Rutherford's gold foil experiment in the light of the structure of the atom.

Aim: To investigate how positive charge and mass are arranged inside an atom.

Apparatus & procedure: Rutherford (with Geiger and Marsden, 1911) directed a fine beam of fast-moving, positively charged alpha (α) particles at a very thin gold foil. A movable fluorescent zinc sulphide screen around the foil produced a flash (scintillation) wherever an alpha particle struck, so the particles could be tracked.

Observations:

  • Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil undeflected.
  • A few were deflected through small angles.
  • A very small number (about 1 in 20,000) bounced straight back (deflected through 90° or more).

Conclusions (structure of the atom):

  • Since most particles passed straight through, an atom is mostly empty space.
  • The few large deflections showed that the positive charge and almost all the mass are concentrated in a very small, dense central region — the nucleus.
  • The nucleus is positively charged (it repels the positive alpha particles) and extremely small compared with the whole atom.
  • The electrons revolve around the nucleus in the empty space outside it.

This led to Rutherford's nuclear (planetary) model of the atom.

Q2. Write down the applications of isotopes in daily life.

1. Medicine (diagnosis & treatment):

  • Cobalt-60 and Iodine-131 gamma rays are used to treat cancer (radiotherapy) and to study/treat the thyroid gland.
  • Sodium-24 is used to trace the flow of blood and detect blood clots.
  • Gamma-emitting isotopes are used to sterilise surgical instruments and syringes.

2. Carbon dating (archaeology): The radioactive isotope Carbon-14 is used to estimate the age of ancient plants, fossils, wood, and bones.

3. Agriculture: Radioisotopes are used to develop better crop varieties, to study how plants take up fertilisers, and to kill pests and preserve food.

4. Industry: Isotopes detect cracks and flaws in metal pipes, welds, and machine parts, and measure the thickness of paper, plastic, and metal sheets.

5. Energy: The isotope Uranium-235 is used as nuclear fuel to generate electricity in nuclear power plants.

6. Research: Isotopes act as tracers to follow the path of elements through chemical reactions and biological processes (e.g. photosynthesis).

Q3. Explain how Bohr's atomic model is different from Rutherford's atomic model.
Rutherford's Model Bohr's Model
Electrons revolve around the nucleus in orbits of any radius/energy. Electrons revolve only in certain fixed, permitted orbits (energy levels).
A revolving electron continuously radiates energy. An electron in an allowed orbit does not radiate energy (stationary states).
Predicts the atom is unstable (electron spirals into nucleus). Explains that the atom is stable.
Cannot explain the line spectrum of hydrogen. Explains the line spectrum: light is emitted/absorbed when an electron jumps between orbits.
Orbit energy is not quantised. Orbit energy is quantised (angular momentum = nh/2π).
Q4. Prove that De Broglie's modern theory is related to Einstein's and Planck's equations.

De Broglie combined two famous equations to find the wavelength of a moving particle.

1. Planck's equation — energy of a photon (wave nature of light):

E = hν = hc / λ   (since c = νλ)

2. Einstein's equation — energy of a particle (particle nature, mass–energy):

E = mc²

3. De Broglie equated the two energies (a particle behaving as a wave has one energy):

mc² = hc / λ
⇒ mc = h / λ
⇒ λ = h / mc

For a particle moving with velocity v (instead of c):
λ = h / mv

This de Broglie equation (λ = h/mv) is built directly from Planck's E = hc/λ and Einstein's E = mc². It proves matter has a dual (wave–particle) nature and links the modern theory to both earlier equations.

Q5. How are cathode rays produced? What are their major characteristics?

Production: Cathode rays are produced in a discharge tube — a sealed glass tube fitted with two electrodes (a cathode and an anode). When the air inside is pumped out to very low pressure and a high voltage is applied, a stream of rays travels from the cathode (negative electrode) towards the anode. These are called cathode rays.

Major characteristics:

  • They travel in straight lines (they cast a sharp shadow of an object placed in their path).
  • They are made of negatively charged particles (electrons) — they are deflected towards the positive plate in an electric field and by a magnetic field.
  • They have mass and momentum — they can rotate a small paddle wheel placed in their path.
  • They produce heat and cause certain materials (like ZnS) to fluoresce/glow.
  • Their properties (e/m ratio) are the same whatever gas or cathode metal is used, proving electrons are a basic part of all matter.
  • They can produce X-rays when they strike a heavy metal target.
Q6. Describe the Schrödinger atomic model.

Erwin Schrödinger (1926) gave the modern (wave-mechanical / quantum-mechanical) model of the atom. It treats the electron as a wave rather than a particle moving in a fixed orbit.

Main ideas:

  • He set up a mathematical wave equation to describe the behaviour of the electron in an atom.
  • Because of Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, we cannot know both the exact position and speed of an electron at the same time. So we speak of the probability of finding an electron in a region.
  • The region around the nucleus where there is the maximum probability of finding an electron is called an orbital (this replaces Bohr's fixed circular orbit).
  • Orbitals have definite shapes and energies: s orbitals are spherical, p orbitals are dumb-bell shaped, and d and f orbitals have more complex shapes.
  • Solving the equation gives the quantum numbers that describe the size, shape, and orientation of each orbital and the spin of the electron.

This model successfully explains the arrangement of electrons in all atoms, not just hydrogen.

Q7. Describe briefly the experiments which provide clues and evidence of the electron, proton, and neutron in an atom.

1. Electron — Discharge tube / cathode rays (J.J. Thomson, 1897): Passing a high voltage through a gas at low pressure produced cathode rays that travelled from cathode to anode. They were deflected towards the positive plate, proving they were negatively charged particles. Their e/m ratio was the same for every gas, so this particle — the electron — is present in all atoms.

2. Proton — Positive (canal) rays (Goldstein, 1886; studied by Rutherford): Using a discharge tube with a perforated cathode, Goldstein observed rays travelling in the opposite direction (through the holes/canals). These were positively charged. With hydrogen gas the lightest positive particle was obtained, and it was named the proton.

3. Neutron — Beryllium bombardment (James Chadwick, 1932): Chadwick bombarded a beryllium sheet with alpha particles and obtained a highly penetrating radiation that was not deflected by electric or magnetic fields — showing the particles had no charge but a mass almost equal to a proton. This neutral particle was named the neutron.
