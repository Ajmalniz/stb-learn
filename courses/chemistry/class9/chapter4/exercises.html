Section A — Multiple Choice Questions

Tick mark (✓) the correct answer.

1. An example of an ionic compound is:
A. H₂
B. CH₄
C. N₂
D. NaCl
NaCl is ionic (metal + non-metal, formed by transfer of electrons). H₂, N₂, and CH₄ are covalent (sharing of electrons).
2. The interaction between a highly electron-deficient hydrogen and a highly electronegative atom is called:
A. Covalent bond
B. Ionic bond
C. Hydrogen bond
D. Metallic bond
A hydrogen bond forms when a δ⁺ hydrogen (bonded to N, O, or F) is attracted to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, or F) of another molecule.
3. Two fluorine atoms share one electron each in their outermost shell to achieve the electronic configuration of:
A. Xe
B. Ar
C. Kr
D. Ne
Fluorine (2, 7) needs 1 more electron. By sharing one electron each, both atoms get 8 valence electrons — the configuration of the nearest noble gas, neon (Ne).
4. The number of electrons lost by atoms of group IIIA equals:
A. 1
B. 2
C. 3
D. 4
Group IIIA elements have 3 valence electrons, so they lose 3 electrons to form +3 ions (e.g. Al → Al³⁺).
5. The atom which loses two electrons from its outer shell to form an ion is:
A. Oxygen
B. Potassium
C. Magnesium
D. Carbon
Magnesium (2, 8, 2) has 2 valence electrons. It loses both to form Mg²⁺ and reach the stable Ne configuration.
6. In an NaCl crystal lattice, each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by:
A. 6 Cl⁻ ions
B. 6 Na⁺ ions
C. 8 Cl⁻ ions
D. 12 Cl⁻ ions
In the NaCl lattice each Na⁺ is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by 6 Na⁺ (coordination number = 6).
7. At room temperature, most ionic compounds are:
A. Amorphous solids
B. Crystalline solids
C. Liquids
D. Gases
Ionic compounds form regular 3-D lattices of ions, so at room temperature they are hard crystalline solids.
8. The tendency of atoms to acquire eight electrons in their valence shell is:
A. Octet rule
B. Duplet rule
C. Triplet rule
D. None of the above
The octet rule: atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to get 8 electrons in their outermost shell (a stable noble-gas arrangement).
9. When one atom forms a cation by losing an electron and another forms an anion by accepting that electron, the bond formed between them is:
A. Covalent bond
B. Ionic bond
C. Coordinate covalent bond
D. Hydrogen bond
Complete transfer of electrons produces oppositely charged ions whose electrostatic attraction is an ionic bond.
10. Noble gases are stable because they contain:
A. 4 electrons in valence shell
B. 6 electrons in valence shell
C. 8 electrons in valence shell
D. 10 electrons in valence shell
Noble gases have a complete octet — 8 electrons in the valence shell (except He, which has a full duplet of 2). This makes them stable and unreactive.
11. A bond which involves 3 shared electron pairs is a:
A. Double covalent bond
B. Single covalent bond
C. Triple covalent bond
D. None of the above
Three shared pairs (6 electrons) form a triple covalent bond, e.g. N≡N in nitrogen gas.
12. A non-metal atom forms an anion by:
A. Loss of electrons
B. Gain of electrons
C. Loss of protons
D. Gain of protons
Non-metals gain electrons to complete their octet, becoming negatively charged anions (e.g. Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻).
13. When two identical atoms share electron pairs and exert the same force on each other, the bond formed is:
A. Non-polar covalent bond
B. Polar covalent bond
C. Double covalent bond
D. Coordinate covalent bond
Identical atoms have equal electronegativity, so the shared pair is pulled equally — a non-polar covalent bond (e.g. H₂, Cl₂, O₂).
14. Synthetic resins are used in places where:
A. Electric resistance is required
B. Water resistance is required
C. Adhesion is required
D. Friction is required
Synthetic resins are good electrical insulators, so they are used where electric resistance (insulation) is required.
15. Oxygen belongs to group VIA, so the number of electrons in its valence shell is:
A. 4
B. 2
C. 6
D. 8
The group A number equals the number of valence electrons. Group VIA → 6 valence electrons (oxygen = 2, 6).
16. Electron pairs which are not shared by atoms are called:
A. Electron pairs
B. Lone pairs
C. Bond pairs
D. Shared pairs
Lone pairs (non-bonding pairs) are valence electron pairs that are not involved in bonding, e.g. the two lone pairs on the oxygen atom of water.
17. The strength of intermolecular forces compared with an ionic or covalent bond is:
A. Weaker
B. Stronger
C. Equal
D. None of the above
Intermolecular forces (between molecules) are much weaker than the actual chemical bonds (ionic/covalent) that hold atoms together within a molecule.
18. Ionic crystals have:
A. High melting points
B. Moderate melting points
C. Low melting points
D. None of the above
Strong electrostatic forces throughout the giant ionic lattice need a lot of heat to break, so ionic crystals have high melting points.
19. The bond formed by mutual sharing of electrons is:
A. Ionic bond
B. Coordinate covalent bond
C. Covalent bond
D. Metallic bond
A covalent bond is formed by the mutual sharing of one or more electron pairs between two atoms (each atom contributes electrons).
20. Which of the following diagrams shows atoms bonded with the same electronegativity?
A. A —•— B  (shared pair exactly in the middle)
B. A ——•— B  (shared pair shifted towards B)
C. A ׃— B
D. A —•— B
Equal electronegativity means the shared electron pair is pulled equally and sits exactly midway between the two atoms (a non-polar bond). The diagram with the electron pair centred between A and B is correct; a diagram showing the pair shifted towards one atom would be polar.

Section B — Short Questions

Q1. Draw dot-and-cross diagrams to show how bonds form when fluorine reacts with (a) Hydrogen and (b) Potassium.

(a) Hydrogen + Fluorine → HF (covalent bond): Hydrogen (1 electron) and fluorine (2, 7) each share one electron, forming one shared pair. Both reach a stable configuration (H → duplet, F → octet).

       ••       
H (×•) F   →   H–F
       •• ••
(× = H's electron, • = F's electrons; one shared pair between H and F, three lone pairs on F)

(b) Potassium + Fluorine → KF (ionic bond): Potassium (2, 8, 8, 1) transfers its 1 valence electron to fluorine (2, 7). This gives K⁺ and F⁻, held by electrostatic attraction.

K• + F(••••••×?) → [K]⁺  [ ••F•• ]⁻  (F now has 8 electrons)
K loses 1 e⁻ → K⁺ ; F gains 1 e⁻ → F⁻
Q2. What is meant by the octet and duplet rule?

Octet rule: Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons so as to have 8 electrons in their outermost (valence) shell, giving them the stable configuration of a noble gas. Example: Na loses 1 e⁻, Cl gains 1 e⁻ — both reach an octet.

Duplet rule: The lightest atoms (H, He, Li) become stable with just 2 electrons in their first/only shell (like helium). Example: in H₂, each hydrogen shares to reach 2 electrons.

Q3. Can you draw an ion which is formed by an atom losing three electrons?

Yes — aluminium (Al) forms such an ion. Aluminium (2, 8, 3) loses its 3 valence electrons to reach the stable neon configuration (2, 8):

Al (2, 8, 3)  →  Al³⁺ (2, 8)  +  3e⁻

The resulting Al³⁺ ion has 13 protons but only 10 electrons, giving it a +3 charge.

Q4. How does oxygen form an anion?

Oxygen (2, 6) has 6 valence electrons and needs 2 more to complete its octet. It gains 2 electrons to form the oxide ion:

O (2, 6)  +  2e⁻  →  O²⁻ (2, 8)

The O²⁻ ion has 8 protons and 10 electrons, so it carries a −2 charge and has the stable neon configuration.

Q6. Explain why table salt has a very high melting point.

Table salt (NaCl) is an ionic compound. Its Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are arranged in a giant three-dimensional lattice held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction acting in all directions.

To melt the salt, a very large amount of heat energy is needed to overcome these strong forces and free the ions. Therefore NaCl has a very high melting point (≈ 801 °C).

Q7. How is the electronegativity value used to determine the formation of a chemical bond?

The difference in electronegativity (ΔEN) between two bonded atoms decides the type of bond:

  • ΔEN = 0 (identical atoms) → non-polar covalent bond (e.g. H₂, Cl₂).
  • ΔEN small (about 0.1–1.7) → polar covalent bond (e.g. HCl, H₂O).
  • ΔEN large (about 1.7 or more) → ionic bond (electron is transferred, e.g. NaCl).

So by comparing electronegativity values we can predict whether a bond will be ionic, polar covalent, or non-polar covalent.

Q8. Why is it easy for a magnesium atom to lose two electrons?

Magnesium (2, 8, 2) has only 2 electrons in its outermost shell. By losing these 2 electrons it reaches the stable configuration of neon (2, 8).

These 2 valence electrons are relatively far from the nucleus and are removed with fairly low ionization energy. Losing them makes magnesium stable, so it easily forms the Mg²⁺ ion.

Q9. Atoms of metallic elements can form ionic bonds, but they are not very good at forming covalent bonds. Why?

Metals have low electronegativity and only a few valence electrons that are loosely held. Their natural tendency is to lose these electrons and form positive ions (cations).

Because they lose electrons rather than share them, metals readily form ionic bonds with non-metals. Covalent bonding requires sharing of electrons between atoms of similar (high) electronegativity, which metals do not do well — so they are poor at forming covalent bonds.

Q11. Describe dipole–dipole forces.

Dipole–dipole forces are attractions between polar molecules. A polar molecule has a positive end (δ⁺) and a negative end (δ⁻) because of unequal sharing of electrons.

The δ⁺ end of one molecule attracts the δ⁻ end of a neighbouring molecule. These forces are weaker than covalent/ionic bonds but stronger than London dispersion forces. Example: HCl molecules attract each other by dipole–dipole forces.

Q12. Write the uses of adhesive material.
  • Joining/sticking wood, paper, leather, plastic, glass, and metals (glues, gum).
  • Epoxy resins are used to bond metals, repair machinery, and in strong construction joints.
  • Used in furniture making, book-binding, packaging, and footwear industry.
  • In electronics for fixing components; in dentistry for fillings; in cars and aircraft to bond parts instead of welding.
Q13. Why are intermolecular forces weaker than intramolecular forces?

Intramolecular forces are the actual chemical bonds (ionic, covalent) that hold atoms together inside a molecule. They involve strong sharing or transfer of electrons.

Intermolecular forces act between whole molecules and are only weak attractions between partial charges or temporary dipoles — no electrons are transferred or shared. Since much less energy is involved, intermolecular forces are much weaker.

Q14. Write the characteristics of a metallic bond.
  • It is the force of attraction between positive metal ions (kernels) and a "sea" of delocalised (free) electrons.
  • The free electrons move throughout the metal, so metals are good conductors of heat and electricity.
  • It is non-directional and gives metals malleability and ductility (they can be hammered and drawn into wires).
  • Strong metallic bonds give most metals high melting and boiling points and a shiny (lustrous) surface.
Q15. Ionic bonds are strong and hard to break, but why do most covalent compounds have low melting and boiling points?

An ionic compound is a giant lattice in which strong electrostatic forces act between all the ions, so a lot of energy is needed to break it — high melting/boiling points.

A covalent compound usually exists as separate small molecules. The bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the molecules are held to each other only by weak intermolecular forces. Little energy is needed to separate the molecules, so most covalent compounds have low melting and boiling points.

Q16. Write down the characteristics of ionic compounds.
  • They are hard, crystalline solids at room temperature.
  • They have high melting and boiling points (strong electrostatic forces).
  • They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water (ions become free to move), but not when solid.
  • Most are soluble in water but insoluble in organic solvents.
  • They are brittle and break along planes when hit.
Q17. Why are ionic compounds solid?

In an ionic compound, oppositely charged ions are packed in a regular 3-D lattice and held by strong electrostatic forces acting in every direction. These forces lock the ions firmly in fixed positions so they can only vibrate, not move freely. This rigid, tightly bound structure makes ionic compounds solid at room temperature.

Q18. How does hydrogen bonding affect the physical properties of compounds?

Hydrogen bonding is an extra force of attraction between molecules, so it raises the energy needed to separate them. Its effects:

  • Gives abnormally high melting and boiling points (e.g. water boils at 100 °C, much higher than expected for its size).
  • Increases viscosity and surface tension (e.g. water forms droplets).
  • Makes substances more soluble in water (e.g. alcohols, sugar).
  • Ice is less dense than water (its open hydrogen-bonded structure) so ice floats.

Section C — Detailed Questions

Q1. Define ionic bond. Discuss the formation of sodium chloride (NaCl).

Ionic bond: The electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed by the complete transfer of electrons from a metal atom to a non-metal atom.

Formation of NaCl:

  • Sodium (2, 8, 1) has 1 valence electron. It loses this electron to become Na⁺ (2, 8) — a stable neon configuration.
  • Chlorine (2, 8, 7) has 7 valence electrons. It gains 1 electron to become Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8) — a stable argon configuration.
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl

The oppositely charged Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions attract each other strongly, forming an ionic bond. Many such ions pack into a giant crystal lattice of sodium chloride.

Q2. Explain how elements attain stability.

Atoms are stable when their outermost shell is completely filled like a noble gas (octet of 8, or duplet of 2 for the smallest atoms). Atoms attain this stability in three ways:

  • By losing electrons: Metals lose their few valence electrons to form cations (e.g. Na → Na⁺, Mg → Mg²⁺).
  • By gaining electrons: Non-metals gain electrons to complete the octet, forming anions (e.g. Cl → Cl⁻, O → O²⁻).
  • By sharing electrons: Non-metals share electron pairs to complete their octets, forming covalent bonds (e.g. H₂, Cl₂, H₂O).

In every case the atom ends up with a stable noble-gas electronic configuration.

Q3. Describe the formation of a covalent bond between two non-metallic atoms. Explain single, double, and triple covalent bonds with examples.

A covalent bond forms when two non-metal atoms share one or more pairs of electrons so that each atom completes its octet (or duplet). Both atoms attract the shared electrons, holding them together.

Single covalent bond (1 shared pair): Example — hydrogen (H₂): H–H. Also Cl₂ (Cl–Cl), HCl.

Double covalent bond (2 shared pairs): Example — oxygen (O₂): O=O. Each oxygen shares 2 electrons so both reach an octet. Also CO₂ (O=C=O).

Triple covalent bond (3 shared pairs): Example — nitrogen (N₂): N≡N. Each nitrogen shares 3 electrons. Also ethyne C₂H₂ (H–C≡C–H).

Q4. How are electrons arranged in a molecular compound? Draw electron dot-and-cross structures for: (a) H₂O (b) N₂ (c) CH₄ (d) C₂H₂ (e) Cl₂ (f) H₂.

In a molecular compound, non-metal atoms share electron pairs so that every atom achieves a stable octet (or duplet for H). Shared pairs form the bonds; unshared pairs remain as lone pairs.

Molecule Bonding Structure

Q5. Define a metallic bond. How are metallic bonds formed?

Metallic bond: The force of attraction between positively charged metal ions (kernels) and the "sea" of freely moving (delocalised) valence electrons that surrounds them.

Formation: Metal atoms have low ionization energy, so their loosely held valence electrons leave the individual atoms and move freely throughout the metal. This turns the atoms into positive ions embedded in a common pool of mobile electrons. The strong attraction between the positive ions and this electron sea is the metallic bond.

This model explains why metals conduct electricity and heat, are malleable and ductile, and have high melting points.

Q6. What is a coordinate covalent bond? Explain with two examples.

A coordinate covalent (dative) bond is a covalent bond in which the shared pair of electrons is donated by only one atom (the donor). The other atom (the acceptor) only provides an empty orbital. Once formed, it is the same as an ordinary covalent bond. It is shown by an arrow (→) from donor to acceptor.

Example 1 — Ammonium ion (NH₄⁺): In NH₃, nitrogen has a lone pair. It donates this lone pair to an H⁺ ion (which has no electrons), forming the fourth N–H bond: H₃N → H⁺ gives NH₄⁺.

Example 2 — Hydronium ion (H₃O⁺): The oxygen of water donates one of its lone pairs to an H⁺ ion, forming H₃O⁺ (H₂O → H⁺).

Q7. What do you understand about the ionic character of a covalent bond?

When two different atoms form a covalent bond, they usually have different electronegativities. The more electronegative atom pulls the shared electron pair closer to itself, gaining a partial negative charge (δ⁻), while the other atom becomes partially positive (δ⁺).

This partial separation of charge means the bond is not purely covalent — it has some ionic character and is called a polar covalent bond. The greater the electronegativity difference, the greater the ionic character.

Example: In HCl the bond is covalent, but because Cl is more electronegative than H, it has significant ionic character (Hδ+–Clδ−).

Q9. Explain the importance of glues and epoxy resins in our society.

Glues (adhesives) and epoxy resins are extremely useful synthetic materials:

  • Joining materials: They bond wood, metal, glass, plastic, ceramics, and paper firmly — often replacing nails, screws, and welding.
  • Construction & repair: Epoxy resins make very strong, waterproof joints used in buildings, bridges, boats, and to repair cracked machine parts.
  • Electrical industry: Epoxy resins are good insulators, so they are used to coat and protect electronic components and circuit boards.
  • Protective coatings: Epoxy paints and varnishes resist water, chemicals, and corrosion, protecting metal surfaces, floors, and pipelines.
  • Everyday & industry use: Used in furniture, packaging, footwear, cars, aircraft, and even in dentistry and medicine.

Thus glues and epoxy resins save labour and cost, make strong lightweight joints, and are essential in modern industry and daily life.
