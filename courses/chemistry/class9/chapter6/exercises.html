Complete solved exercises from Sindh Textbook Board Chapter 6 — Solutions. Sections A, B, C, and D (numericals on molarity & concentration) fully solved for exam preparation.

Section A — Multiple Choice Questions

Tick mark (✓) the correct answer.

1. An alloy is the homogeneous mixture of:
A. Two solids
B. Two liquids
C. Two gases
D. Solid and liquid
An alloy is a solid solution — a homogeneous mixture of two or more solids (metals), e.g. brass = copper + zinc.
2. A saturated solution of KCl on heating becomes:
A. Unsaturated
B. Supersaturated
C. Diluted
D. All of these
Heating increases solubility, so the solution can now dissolve more solute — it becomes unsaturated.
3. If we dissolve sand into water, then the mixture is said to be:
A. Solution
B. Suspension
C. Colloids
D. Concentrated solution
Sand particles are large and do not dissolve; they remain suspended and settle down, so the mixture is a suspension.
4. Solubility is usually expressed in grams of the solute dissolved in ______ grams of a solvent:
A. 10
B. 100
C. 500
D. 1000
Solubility is defined as the number of grams of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent to make a saturated solution at a given temperature.
5. Example of a heterogeneous mixture is:
A. Sugar and water
B. Sand and water
C. Salt and water
D. Ink and water
Sand and water is heterogeneous — the sand does not dissolve and you can see two separate phases. Sugar, salt, and ink dissolve to give homogeneous mixtures.
6. 2 moles of sodium chloride (NaCl) is equal to:
A. 123 grams
B. 135 grams
C. 158 grams
D. 117 grams
Molar mass of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g/mol. Mass = moles × molar mass = 2 × 58.5 = 117 g.
7. The molarity of a solution which is prepared by dissolving 40 g sodium chloride in 500 cm³ of solution is:
A. 1.4 M
B. 1.5 M
C. 1.6 M
D. 1.7 M
Moles of NaCl = 40 ÷ 58.5 = 0.684 mol. Volume = 500 cm³ = 0.5 dm³. Molarity = 0.684 ÷ 0.5 = 1.37 ≈ 1.4 M.
8. 10% (w/w) sugar solution means that 10 grams of solute is dissolved in:
A. 90 g of water
B. 95 g of water
C. 100 g of water
D. 105 g of water
10% w/w means 10 g solute in 100 g of solution. So water (solvent) = 100 − 10 = 90 g.
9. An example of a true solution is:
A. Solution of starch
B. Solution of soap
C. Ink in water
D. Tooth paste
A true solution has very small (dissolved) particles and is transparent. Ink in water is a true solution, while starch and soap solutions are colloids and toothpaste is a suspension.
10. Which solution contains more water?
A. 1.0 M
B. 0.75 M
C. 0.5 M
D. 0.25 M
The lower the molarity, the more dilute the solution. 0.25 M has the least solute, so it contains the most water.
11. When a saturated solution is diluted, it changes into:
A. Saturated solution
B. Unsaturated solution
C. Concentrated solution
D. Supersaturated solution
Adding more solvent (dilution) means the solution can now hold more solute, so it becomes unsaturated.
12. Butter is an example of a solution of type:
A. Gas–liquid
B. Solid–liquid
C. Liquid–solid
D. Gas–solid
In butter, tiny droplets of liquid (fat/water) are dispersed in a solid medium — a liquid-in-solid system.
13. A solution that contains a solid solute in a liquid solvent is called:
A. Solids in gas
B. Liquids in solids
C. Solids in solids
D. Solids in liquid
A solid dissolved in a liquid (e.g. salt/sugar in water) is a solid-in-liquid solution.
14. What is the particle size in a suspension?
A. 10³ nm
B. 10² nm
C. Less than 10³ nm
D. Greater than 10³ nm
Suspension particles are the largest — greater than 10³ nm (1000 nm) — so they are visible and settle down on standing.

Section B — Short Questions

Q1. Explain the solute–solvent interaction to prepare a sodium chloride solution.

When NaCl (solute) is added to water (solvent), the polar water molecules surround the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. The negative (oxygen) end of water attracts the Na⁺ ions and the positive (hydrogen) end attracts the Cl⁻ ions.

These attractions (ion–dipole forces) pull the ions out of the crystal lattice and spread them uniformly through the water — the salt dissolves. Each dissolved ion becomes surrounded by water molecules (hydration), giving a clear sodium chloride solution.

Q2. Differentiate between a saturated and an unsaturated solution.
Saturated Solution Unsaturated Solution
Contains the maximum solute it can dissolve at that temperature.Contains less solute than it can dissolve.
No more solute dissolves (extra solute settles).More solute can still be dissolved.
In equilibrium with undissolved solute.Not in equilibrium; no excess solute.
Q3. Define a solution and explain the major components of a solution.

Solution: A homogeneous mixture of two or more substances whose composition is uniform throughout.

Major components:

  • Solute: the substance present in the smaller amount, which dissolves (e.g. salt).
  • Solvent: the substance present in the larger amount, which does the dissolving (e.g. water).

Example: in a salt solution, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.

Q4. What do you mean by mass/volume percent?

Mass/volume percent (% m/v) is the mass of solute (in grams) dissolved in 100 cm³ of solution.

% m/v = (mass of solute in g ÷ volume of solution in cm³) × 100

Example: a 5% m/v glucose solution contains 5 g of glucose in every 100 cm³ of solution.

Q5. Define, with an example, one molar solution.

A one molar (1 M) solution is one that contains one mole of solute dissolved in one dm³ (1 litre) of solution.

Example: dissolving 1 mole of NaOH (40 g) in enough water to make exactly 1 dm³ of solution gives a 1 M NaOH solution.

Q6. Why does a colloid show the Tyndall effect?

A colloid contains particles that are large enough to scatter light (but still too small to see individually). When a beam of light passes through a colloid, these dispersed particles scatter the light, making the path of the beam visible. This scattering of light by colloidal particles is called the Tyndall effect (e.g. the beam of a torch visible in fog or through milk). In a true solution the particles are too small to scatter light, so no Tyndall effect is seen.

Q7. Define the terms: (i) Dilution (ii) Concentration (iii) Solubility (iv) Molarity.

(i) Dilution: The process of adding more solvent to a solution to decrease its concentration.

(ii) Concentration: The amount of solute present in a given amount of solvent or solution.

(iii) Solubility: The maximum amount of solute (in grams) that dissolves in 100 g of solvent at a given temperature to form a saturated solution.

(iv) Molarity: The number of moles of solute dissolved in one dm³ (litre) of solution (units: mol/dm³).

Q8. Polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents only. Why?

Polar solvents (like water) have positive and negative ends. These ends can attract the ions or the charged ends of polar solute molecules strongly enough (ion–dipole and dipole–dipole forces) to pull them apart and surround them.

This attraction overcomes the forces holding the solute together, so polar and ionic solutes dissolve. This follows the rule "like dissolves like."

Q9. Why does a polar solute not dissolve in a non-polar solvent?

A non-polar solvent (like petrol or benzene) has no charged ends, so it cannot attract the ions or the charged ends of a polar solute. The weak forces between a polar solute and a non-polar solvent are not strong enough to break the strong attractions within the polar solute. Therefore the polar solute does not dissolve — again, "like dissolves like."

Q10. How are solutions beneficial for the community?
  • Medicines: many drugs, drips (saline/glucose), syrups, and injections are solutions.
  • Beverages & food: soft drinks, tea, sugar/salt solutions used daily.
  • Body function: digestion, blood, and transport of nutrients all occur in solution.
  • Industry & agriculture: fertilisers, pesticides, dyes, and cleaning agents are used as solutions.
  • Water supply: chlorine solution is used to purify drinking water.
Q11. Why does salt dissolve in water?

Salt (NaCl) is ionic, and water is a polar solvent. The polar water molecules attract the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions — the oxygen (δ⁻) end pulls Na⁺ and the hydrogen (δ⁺) ends pull Cl⁻. These strong ion–dipole attractions pull the ions out of the crystal lattice and surround them (hydration), so the salt dissolves in water.

Q12. Air is a solution containing oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen and other gases. Which one of the gases is called the solvent and why?

Nitrogen is the solvent in air. In a solution the component present in the largest amount is the solvent. Air is about 78% nitrogen — the greatest proportion — so nitrogen acts as the solvent and the other gases (oxygen, CO₂, etc.) are the solutes.

Q13. Why does gasoline not dissolve in water?

Gasoline (petrol) is a non-polar substance, while water is polar. Because "like dissolves like," a non-polar solute cannot be attracted and pulled apart by polar water molecules. The two do not mix, so gasoline does not dissolve in water and instead floats as a separate layer.

Section C — Detailed Questions

Q1. Describe how to prepare a dilute solution from a concentrated solution.

A dilute solution is made by adding more solvent to a concentrated solution. The number of moles of solute stays the same; only the volume increases, so the concentration decreases.

We use the dilution formula:

M₁V₁ = M₂V₂
(M₁, V₁ = molarity & volume before ; M₂, V₂ = after dilution)

Steps: (1) Calculate the volume of concentrated solution needed using the formula. (2) Measure that volume with a pipette. (3) Transfer it to a volumetric flask. (4) Add distilled water up to the required mark and mix well.

Example: To make 100 cm³ of 0.1 M solution from 1 M stock: V₁ = M₂V₂ ÷ M₁ = (0.1 × 100) ÷ 1 = 10 cm³. Take 10 cm³ of the 1 M solution and add water up to 100 cm³.

Q2. Define the term solubility. How do the nature of solute and solvent determine the extent of dissolution?

Solubility: The maximum number of grams of a solute that dissolves in 100 g of a solvent at a given temperature to form a saturated solution.

Effect of nature of solute and solvent ("like dissolves like"):

  • Polar/ionic solutes dissolve well in polar solvents (e.g. salt and sugar dissolve in water).
  • Non-polar solutes dissolve well in non-polar solvents (e.g. grease and oil dissolve in petrol or benzene).
  • A polar solute does not dissolve in a non-polar solvent, and vice versa.

So the extent of dissolution depends on how similar the solute and solvent are in polarity — the more alike, the greater the solubility.

Q3. Why does the solubility of a salt increase with an increase in temperature?

Dissolving most salts is an endothermic process (it absorbs heat). When the temperature is raised, extra heat energy is supplied, which:

  • gives the solvent molecules more kinetic energy, so they move faster and strike the solute more often, and
  • helps break the forces holding the solute particles in the crystal lattice.

As a result, more solute particles are pulled into solution, so the solubility increases with temperature (e.g. more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold).

Q4. Explain the attraction of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions for a water molecule.

A water molecule is polar: the oxygen end is slightly negative (δ⁻) and the hydrogen ends are slightly positive (δ⁺).

  • The Na⁺ ion (positive) is attracted to the negative oxygen (δ⁻) end of water molecules.
  • The Cl⁻ ion (negative) is attracted to the positive hydrogen (δ⁺) ends of water molecules.

These ion–dipole attractions surround each ion with water molecules (hydration), pulling the ions apart from the NaCl crystal and dissolving the salt.

Q5. Explain solubility with reference to the "like dissolves like" principle.

The principle "like dissolves like" means a solute dissolves best in a solvent of similar polarity.

  • Polar/ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents: e.g. NaCl and sugar dissolve in water because water's charged ends attract the solute particles.
  • Non-polar solutes dissolve in non-polar solvents: e.g. oil, grease, and wax dissolve in petrol, benzene, or kerosene.
  • Unlike combinations do not dissolve: oil (non-polar) does not dissolve in water (polar); salt (ionic) does not dissolve in petrol.

This is because dissolving needs the solute–solvent attractions to be strong enough to overcome the forces within the solute — which happens only when the two are alike in polarity.

Section D — Numericals (Solved)

Key formula: Molarity (M) = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution (dm³)  ;  moles = mass ÷ molar mass.  (1 dm³ = 1000 cm³)

Q1. What is the molarity of the solution prepared by dissolving 1.25 g of HCl gas in enough water to make 30 cm³ of solution?
Molar mass of HCl = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5 g/mol
Moles = 1.25 ÷ 36.5 = 0.03425 mol
Volume = 30 cm³ = 0.030 dm³
Molarity = 0.03425 ÷ 0.030 = 1.14 mol/dm³

M ≈ 1.14 M
Q2. A solution of potassium chloride was prepared by dissolving 2.5 g of KCl in water and making the volume up to 100 cm³. Find the concentration of the solution in mol/dm³.
Molar mass of KCl = 39 + 35.5 = 74.5 g/mol
Moles = 2.5 ÷ 74.5 = 0.03356 mol
Volume = 100 cm³ = 0.100 dm³
Concentration = 0.03356 ÷ 0.100 = 0.336 mol/dm³

≈ 0.34 mol/dm³
Q3. A flask contains 0.25 M NaOH solution. What mass of NaOH is present per dm³ of solution?
Molar mass of NaOH = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40 g/mol
Moles in 1 dm³ = 0.25 mol
Mass = moles × molar mass = 0.25 × 40 = 10 g

10 g of NaOH per dm³
Q4. What volume of 0.5 M acid is needed to neutralize 200 ml of 4 M base?

For a 1 : 1 neutralization, moles of acid = moles of base, so MaVa = MbVb.

M_a × V_a = M_b × V_b
0.5 × V_a = 4 × 200
V_a = 800 ÷ 0.5 = 1600 ml

V_a = 1600 ml = 1.6 dm³
Q5. A mineral water bottle contains 28 mg of calcium in 100 cm³ of solution. What is the concentration in g/dm³?
Mass = 28 mg = 0.028 g
Volume = 100 cm³ = 0.100 dm³
Concentration = 0.028 ÷ 0.100 = 0.28 g/dm³

0.28 g/dm³
Q6. A solution of 20 cm³ of alcohol is dissolved in 80 cm³ of water. Calculate the concentration (v/v) of this solution.
% v/v = (volume of solute ÷ volume of solution) × 100
Volume of solution = 20 + 80 = 100 cm³
% v/v = (20 ÷ 100) × 100 = 20%

Concentration = 20% (v/v)
Q7. How much sodium hydroxide (NaOH) is required to prepare 400 cm³ of a 0.3 M solution?
Molar mass of NaOH = 40 g/mol
Volume = 400 cm³ = 0.400 dm³
Moles = M × V = 0.3 × 0.400 = 0.12 mol
Mass = moles × molar mass = 0.12 × 40 = 4.8 g

4.8 g of NaOH
